You research facts for a short-video channel whose whole promise is: things you have never heard before, about things you already know.

List 15 different facts from the topic area. Spread them across different subjects; no two facts about the same animal, object or event.

Each fact must be:
- about something every adult already knows (an everyday animal, body part, food, object, place, weather, a famous person or event), so it needs no background knowledge;
- genuinely surprising or counter-intuitive: it should overturn something people assume, or reveal something hidden in plain sight;
- stated in one plain sentence that a 12-year-old understands, with no technical words;
- true and confirmed by mainstream sources such as textbooks, major museums, universities or peer-reviewed research. No myths, no disputed claims, no "scientists think" guesses, no records that change every year.

Stay far away from famous trivia. If a fact appears in listicles, viral posts, quiz shows, children's fact books or school lessons, it is useless to us. Examples of facts that are TOO WELL KNOWN: wombats make cube-shaped droppings; octopuses have three hearts; honey never spoils; bananas are berries; sharks are older than trees; a day on Venus is longer than its year; lightning is hotter than the Sun; clouds weigh hundreds of tonnes; there are more trees than stars in the galaxy; the Great Pyramid has eight sides; Cleopatra lived closer to the Moon landing than to the pyramids; flamingos are pink from their food; sloths, axolotls, tardigrades and mantis shrimp facts of any kind. Look instead for the kind of detail found deep inside a science book, a museum label or a research paper.

Then rate every fact honestly, as a sceptical editor would:
- wow, 1 to 10: would an ordinary person say "no way" and repeat it to a friend? 9 to 10 = jaw-dropping. How things are manufactured, what parts are called, legal definitions, laboratory tricks and classroom science experiments (things floating, surface tension, static electricity, magnets, optical illusions) are 1 to 3; we do not want those.
- familiarity, 1 to 10: how likely an average adult has already heard it. 1 = almost nobody has, 5 = trivia fans know it, 10 = everybody knows it. Be harsh: if you have seen it repeated often, it is at least a 6.
- certainty, 1 to 10: how sure you are that it is true exactly as stated. 10 only for facts you could cite.
- filmable, 1 to 10: can the surprising thing itself be SEEN in plain video of the subject? 9 to 10 = a camera pointed at the subject shows it (an animal doing something, an object behaving oddly, a striking place). 5 or lower if the subject is the human body or people (video of people looks fake), or if the surprise is a number, a date, a comparison, something microscopic or inside a body, a precise pattern of light or shadow, or if showing it needs human hands, faces, crowds, diagrams, maps or text.

In "why", give the explanation in one or two plain sentences, including one concrete number or name if there is one.
