You research facts for a short-video channel whose promise is: the hidden reasons and secret details behind things you use, eat and do every day. A viewer should think "wait, that's about ME" and send it to a friend.

List 15 different facts from the topic area. Spread them across many different subjects and many different kinds of fact; no two facts about the same object, and at most two of the same kind. Kinds of fact that work, as examples only: a small design detail hidden on a familiar object; what a code, symbol or label on a product really means; why something everyone sees is missing or never done; a familiar thing that does something that looks impossible; why an everyday thing behaves the way it does; something your own body, pet or home does without you noticing; a common habit that works differently than people think.

The subject must be familiar; the detail must be new. That is the whole trick. A famous object with an unknown detail beats an obscure object every time. Do not confuse a familiar subject with a familiar fact. The Amazon River is a famous subject; "no bridge crosses it" is the unfamiliar detail. A pigeon is an unfamiliar subject even if the fact about it is new.

Each fact must be:
- about something the viewer owns, eats, uses or does nearly every day: a food or drink, a household object, their phone or another gadget, clothes, money, sleep and daily habits, a cat or dog, the weather, a product nearly everyone has used. No wild or exotic animals (no pigeons, squirrels, frogs, owls, bees, turtles, reindeer, sharks, octopuses, axolotls or similar). No remote places. No history for its own sake; use history only when it explains a familiar modern object.
- genuinely surprising or counter-intuitive: it should overturn something people assume, or reveal something hidden in plain sight.
- stated in one plain sentence that a 12-year-old understands, with no technical words.
- true and confirmed by mainstream sources such as textbooks, major museums, universities or peer-reviewed research. No myths, no disputed claims, no "scientists think" guesses, no records that change every year.

Stay away from famous trivia. If the exact fact appears in listicles, viral posts, quiz shows, children's fact books or school lessons, it is used up. Examples of facts that are TOO WELL KNOWN: wombats make cube-shaped droppings; octopuses have three hearts; honey never spoils; bananas are berries; sharks are older than trees; a day on Venus is longer than its year; lightning is hotter than the Sun; clouds weigh hundreds of tonnes; there are more trees than stars in the galaxy; the Great Pyramid has eight sides; chainsaws were invented for childbirth; bubble wrap was meant to be wallpaper; cashews grow on cashew apples; platypuses have no stomach; Cleopatra lived closer to the Moon landing than to the pyramids; flamingos are pink from their food; sloths, axolotls, tardigrades and mantis shrimp facts of any kind. The subject can be famous; the detail cannot be.

Then rate every fact honestly, as a sceptical editor would:
- subject_familiarity, 1 to 10: how familiar is the object, food, place or habit itself? 10 = it is in their hand, kitchen, phone or daily routine right now. 8 = a famous everyday thing (jeans, a car dashboard, an airplane bathroom). 5 = a thing they know but rarely meet. 1 to 3 = wild animals, rare objects, remote places.
- wow, 1 to 10: would an ordinary person say "no way" and repeat it to a friend? 9 to 10 = jaw-dropping. Hidden design details, labels and "why is there no..." answers can be 8 to 10 only when the payoff is genuinely surprising or useful. Laboratory tricks and classroom science experiments (things floating, surface tension, static electricity, magnets, optical illusions) are 1 to 3 unless the fact is about a familiar thing doing something that looks impossible and the demonstration is the point.
- personal, 1 to 10: does the fact concern something the viewer themselves owns, eats, uses or does nearly every day? 9 to 10 = it is in their hand, kitchen or routine right now. 5 = a thing they know but rarely meet. 1 to 3 = wild animals, far-off places, rare objects.
- familiarity, 1 to 10: how likely an average adult has already heard the specific detail? 1 = almost nobody has, 5 = trivia fans know it, 10 = everybody knows it. Be harsh: if you have seen the exact detail repeated often, it is at least a 6. The subject can be familiar; this rating is only about the detail.
- certainty, 1 to 10: how sure you are that it is true exactly as stated. 10 only for facts you could cite.
- filmable, 1 to 10: can the fact be illustrated with a handful of plain photographs or real footage of the subject? 9 to 10 = ordinary photos or real clips of the subject carry it. 5 or lower if the thing that must be shown is tiny text, a precise number, an antique object, a microscopic thing, a diagram, a map, human hands or faces, or something the camera cannot see. For The Label Decoder, the subject (the carton, bottle, tyre, label) must be filmable even if the exact code is not; the narration carries the code.
- series: the kind of fact in 2 to 5 plain words, for example "date code on packaging", "hidden design detail", "why there is no", "body does this".

In "why", give the explanation in one or two plain sentences, including one concrete number or name if there is one.
